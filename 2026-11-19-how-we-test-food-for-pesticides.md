When a news report says "pesticide residues were found in vegetables", what happened in the laboratory? Here is how a typical analysis works in our Food Safety Laboratory.

## 1. Sampling

We buy or collect samples following a plan, so results represent what people actually eat. Each sample is labelled, chopped and blended into a uniform paste.

## 2. Extraction with QuEChERS

QuEChERS stands for *Quick, Easy, Cheap, Effective, Rugged and Safe*. A small portion of the blended sample is shaken with acetonitrile, a solvent that dissolves most pesticides. Salts are then added to separate the solvent from the water in the food.

## 3. Clean-up

Food contains fats, pigments and sugars that interfere with measurement. A small amount of sorbent is mixed with the extract to absorb these, leaving a cleaner solution.

## 4. Measurement

The extract goes into a chromatograph, such as our UHPLC, which separates compounds as they travel through a column. A detector then identifies and measures each one. Every compound leaves the column at a characteristic time, which appears as a peak on a chromatogram.

## 5. Quality control

Before testing real samples, we validate the method: we add known amounts of pesticide to clean food and check that we recover them accurately and consistently. Blanks and spiked samples are run alongside every batch.

## 6. Interpreting the result

A residue is compared with the **maximum residue limit (MRL)**, the legal limit for that pesticide in that food. A result above the MRL means a rule was broken. It does not automatically mean the food is harmful. For that, scientists carry out a **risk assessment**, which compares how much people eat with toxicologically safe intake levels.
